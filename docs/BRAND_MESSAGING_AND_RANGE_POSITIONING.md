# Emotional positioning: Aeos, and the range beneath it

Research and build plan — 7 November 2026.
Scope: what premium makers do to feel opulent, and how JTH should name and sell
the second range without calling it cheap.

---

## The short answer

Three findings, in order of importance.

**1. Nobody in this category sells on price, and nobody apologises for the
cheaper line.** Every maker researched names its accessible range after a
*virtue* or a *job*, and explains the price gap with a difference of *method* —
never a difference of quality. Bloomfields calls its entry 3.5t the "ultimate
work box". Stephex calls its accessible range "functional", its flagship
"luxurious". Böckmann's small truck "feels like a car". deVOL's cheaper kitchen
is "the quintessential deVOL kitchen" — the *defining* one, not the lesser one.

**2. The word is "Essential", and the mechanism is "coachbuilt vs. proven
layouts".** Aeos is drawn around the customer. The second range is built to
layouts JTH has already refined over hundreds of boxes. That is a real,
provable, non-apologetic reason for a lower price, and it is the same mechanism
deVOL uses (bespoke vs. modular) and British Standard used (fewer options).
Price is described as "a more modest price" — Hans Wilsdorf's own phrase when he
founded Tudor — never budget, value, entry-level, affordable or cost-effective.

**3. There is a structural problem underneath the question, and it matters more
than the wording.** JTH's own name is currently attached to the cheaper tier
("JTH Principle 45") while the flagship carries an acquired name ("Aeos Edge
45"). Every bit of prestige the flagship earns accrues to *Aeos*, and the house
name reads as the budget label. That is backwards, and no amount of copy fixes
it. JTH has to become the maker's mark that sits above both ranges — the way
deVOL sits above Classic English and Real Shaker — not the name of the cheap one.

## Part 1 — Eight signals that produce opulence

Each is a mechanism, with what the evidence showed and what JTH should do.

### 1. The horse is the emotional object, not the horsebox

The best sentence found anywhere in the category is on Bloomfields' Eventer 5T
XS page. It talks about your horse being your best friend, your therapy, your
partner and your pride — and never mentions the vehicle at all.

That is the whole category's emotional engine and JTH does not touch it once.
The current site talks about tonnage, payload, GRP and warranties. Those are
*reassurance*, and reassurance is necessary, but it is not desire. Nobody spends
£46,000 on a payload.

**Do:** one piece of copy, high on the homepage, that is about the relationship
between the owner and the horse — the 4am starts, the long drive home after a
good round, the fact that the box is where the horse is calmest. Then let the
engineering follow as proof that JTH takes that seriously.

### 2. Price is explained by method, never by material

This is the single most transferable finding.

- deVOL: the cheaper range is *modular*; standard sizes. They say outright that
  the bespoke range costs around 60% more, and that both are made in the same
  Leicestershire workshop, from similar timbers, with the same joinery and the
  same paint process. Quality parity is asserted explicitly: whichever range you
  pick, same quality.
- British Standard: prices were held down by *limiting options*, with the
  carcass and drawer quality unchanged from Plain English.
- Tudor was founded, in Wilsdorf's words, to sell at a more modest price than
  Rolex while attaining the same standards of dependability.

In every case the concession is to **choice**, not to **substance**.

**Do:** state the mechanism plainly on the site. The Aeos range is coachbuilt to
the customer's drawing; the Essential range is built to layouts JTH has proven.
Same shell, same structure, same workshop, same paint booth, same people —
shorter options list. Then publish the actual price delta rather than hiding it.
Confidence about price *is* a luxury signal; coyness reads as embarrassment.

### 3. The accessible range gets a superlative, not a hedge

Bloomfields' cheapest 3.5t is "the ultimate work box", sold on unrivalled
strength at an untouchable price. No hedging, no "entry-level", no "ideal for
first-time buyers".

Compare the live JTH site, which currently sells the Principle 35 as a "perfect
balance of quality and value", badges it "Best Value", and in the buying guide
targets it at "first-time buyers, budget conscious". That is three separate
diminishing signals on one model, and the last one tells an aspirational buyer
that choosing it marks them out as someone who could not afford more.

**Do:** delete "Best Value", "budget conscious", "Essential features" as a
spec-sheet shrug, and "cost-effective" wherever it appears. Give each model a job
and a virtue instead. A Principle is the box that goes out every weekend of the
season and asks nothing of you.

### 4. Specificity is the texture of luxury; adjectives are the texture of templates

Bloomfields quantify relentlessly: over 15 metres of 50mm aluminium box section,
4 metres of weld, a 160-point quality check, a bulkhead tested past the point
where the test rig ran out of capacity. They name the staff — who you will speak
to, who oversees the builds, who runs quality.

The numbers are doing emotional work, not technical work. They are proof that
somebody cared enough to count.

**Do:** JTH's recent `TrustIndicators` rewrite already understood this — facts
ruled like a maker's plate, not icons over slogans. Push it further. Replace
"advanced GRP construction with aerospace-grade materials" (which the spec sheet
has already flagged as needing evidence) with countable facts JTH can stand
behind. Name the workshop team. Say how many boxes have left Beeston.

### 5. Provenance documents

Purdey issue History Certificates for their guns. It is a small thing that
converts a purchase into an inheritance.

**Do:** a build record for every JTH — the chassis, the build number, the dates,
the names of the people who built it, the specification as delivered, signed.
Handed over with the keys, and reissued free to any future owner. It costs almost
nothing, it is a genuine differentiator in this category, it makes a JTH easier
to resell, and it is the kind of detail the equestrian press writes about.

### 6. The workshop as theatre

Bloomfields actively encourage factory visits and argue that you cannot judge a
horsebox without seeing it at its rawest stage. Their four bays are open to any
potential buyer. deVOL's published explainer keeps returning to the Leicestershire
workshop. Morgan sell themselves as a coachbuilder — an antidote to
mass-production.

JTH already says "come and visit the workshop". It is buried in a contact block
near the bottom of the homepage.

**Do:** promote the workshop visit to a primary call to action, beside "Get a
quote". "See yours being built" is a far stronger invitation than "Get a quote",
and it is a commitment no importer or reseller can match.

### 7. Belonging, and the company you keep

Fairfax & Favor — a Norfolk brand selling to very nearly JTH's customer — frame
the brand as a lifestyle and welcome people to the club. Stephex put their name
on a rider award and lead with journeys you share. Bloomfields collaborated with
Holland Cooper on a limited-edition box, which is a pure status play: two British
brands borrowing each other's taste.

**Do:** owners' photography, named and credited, where the horse and the yard are
the subject. Longer term, this is the strongest argument for a JTH collaboration
or an owners' register.

### 8. Restraint

Morgan's homepage headline is two words. Tudor's manifesto rejects the status
quo in one line. The pattern across every genuinely premium site researched is
*less said, more shown*.

The current JTH homepage carries two large blocks of SEO prose — "Why Choose JTH
3.5t Horseboxes", "Welcome to J Taylor Horseboxes" — written for a crawler and
visibly so, including a US spelling ("specializes") on a site with a UK English
compliance doc. Premium buyers read that as a tell.

**Do:** cut both blocks to a single short statement of what JTH is. Move the
keyword coverage into the model pages and the buying guide, where it belongs and
where it reads as helpful rather than padded.

## Part 2 — The range problem

### What the site currently communicates

`data/pricing.json` shows the two ranges running in parallel at 4.5 tonnes:

| | Aeos | Second range |
|---|---|---|
| 4.5t | Edge 45 (£31,500), Freedom 45 (£36,500), Discovery 45 (£46,100), Edge ST 45 | JTH Principle 45, JTH Professional 45, JTH Progeny 45 |
| 3.5t | — | Principle 35 (£19,500), Professional 35 (£22,000–22,950), Progeny 35 (£25,500) |
| 7.2t / 7.5t | Discovery 72 (£74,600), Helios 75 (£82,500) | — |

So a buyer at 4.5 tonnes chooses between an "Aeos" and a "JTH". The naming makes
JTH the cheaper of two brands rather than the maker of both.

### Why this is the real problem

Tudor can carry Rolex's standards without being Rolex's discount line because
Tudor is a *separate name with its own purpose*. Rolex's name is nowhere on it.
deVOL works the opposite way and just as well: one maker's name over two ranges,
with the cheaper one framed as the quintessential expression of the house.

JTH has accidentally built the one configuration that does not work: **the house
name on the cheap tier, an acquired name on the flagship.** Aeos accumulates the
prestige; JTH accumulates the price objection.

### The fix

Make JTH the maker's mark on everything, and give the second range a range name
that sits *beside* Aeos rather than beneath it.

- Flagship: **Aeos** — "coachbuilt". Drawn around you.
- Second range: **Essential** — the proven layouts. Same build.
- Both: **built by JTH in Beeston, Norfolk.**

In practice that means "Aeos Discovery 45, built by JTH" and "JTH Essential
Principle 45" — or simply "Principle 45, from the JTH Essential range". The
prefix "JTH" stops being a tier and starts being a signature.

This is a brand decision, not a copy decision, and it is yours and James's to
make. The alternatives, if "Essential" does not land:

| Option | Reads as | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Essential** (recommended) | Edited to what matters | Can be heard as "basic" if the parity story is weak |
| **Core** | The heart of the range | Slightly corporate |
| **Classic** | Established, proven | Overused; can imply old |
| **Considered** | Credits the buyer's judgement | Design-world register, may not suit the yard |
| **Working** | Earns its keep; high praise in equestrian use | Sits awkwardly against Aeos's opulence |

### The sentence that does the work

> Every JTH is built to one standard, in one workshop, by the same people. The
> Aeos range is coachbuilt to your drawing. The Essential range is built to the
> layouts we have proven over hundreds of boxes — at a more modest price.

### Lexicon

**Use:** coachbuilt, built to order, proven, specified, layouts, the same
structure, a more modest price, the workshop, built in Beeston, by hand, build
number, hundreds of boxes.

**Never use:** budget, budget-friendly, cheap, cheap and cheerful, value, best
value, cost-effective, entry-level, basic, stripped, no-frills, affordable
luxury, starter.

**Handle with care:** "premium" (ten occurrences on the current homepage; it is a
word brands use about themselves when they have not earned a better one), and
"luxury" (earn it with specifics or drop it).

## Part 3 — The gating issue: the parity claim is not yet true on the site

The whole strategy rests on one claim — *same build, fewer choices*. The site
currently contradicts it, and the master spec sheet has already caught some of it:

1. **Warranty is not at parity.** The homepage product cards give Professional 35
   and Progeny 35 a 2-year structural warranty and Principle 35 twelve months.
   A shorter warranty on the cheaper box is a *material* difference and it
   directly refutes "same build". This has to be resolved before the parity
   language is published. Either the structure genuinely is the same — in which
   case the warranty should match — or it is not, in which case the positioning
   needs to change.
2. **Ramps.** The homepage safety list claims an aluminium-frame ramp with no gap
   and no step; spec sheet row 28 notes the Aeos pages say steel. One of these is
   wrong, and "same structure" cannot be claimed over an unresolved contradiction.
3. **Stallion stall.** Listed as standard on the homepage, "available" on the
   detail pages (row 27).
4. **"Aerospace-grade materials"** (row 42) and the old "twice the industry
   standard" warranty claim need evidence or removal.

Four more contradictions surfaced while doing the Phase 1 copy pass, none of
them yet on the Wording sheet:

5. **The Aeos 4.5t prices look mis-assigned.** On `/models` the Aeos Edge 45,
   Freedom 45 and Discovery 45 *cards* say "Price on application", while the
   buying guide and comparison table on the same page quote £31,500, £36,500 and
   £46,100. The first two figures are also the card prices of the JTH
   Professional 45 and JTH Progeny 45. Either the guide is quoting the JTH 45
   prices against Aeos names, or the cards are missing prices they should have.
   This is the most serious figure problem found so far — it is on the page a
   buyer uses to compare the two ranges.
6. **Principle 35 horse capacity.** The homepage card and the comparison table
   say two horses; the model page says one horse or two ponies.
7. **Principle 35 warranty, stated two ways on one page.** The hero stat says
   "12 Month"; the specification table below says "1 year build warranty".
8. **A parity claim was already live and has been removed.** The Principle 45
   copy claimed it was built with the same British craftsmanship as the premium
   models. Since parity is exactly what rows 22/27/28 leave unresolved, that
   sentence was taken out rather than left standing. It can go back, in stronger
   form, once parity is confirmed — it is the single most valuable sentence on
   that page if it is true.

**Recommendation: do not publish any parity or "same build" language until rows
22, 23, 27, 28 and 42 of the Wording sheet are signed off.** Writing a confident
brand story on top of figures that disagree with each other is how a premium
brand loses a customer at the workshop door, when the buyer notices. The spec
sheet work already in flight is therefore a prerequisite for this plan, not a
parallel task.

### Phase 2 — Emotional layer

**2.1 The horse section.** New section high on `app/(site)/page.tsx`, directly
after the hero — one short paragraph about the owner and the horse, over the best

This is the single biggest gap against Bloomfields.

**2.2 Promote the workshop visit.** `components/HeroAdvanced.tsx` usage on the

**2.3 Cut the SEO prose.** Remove the "Welcome to J Taylor Horseboxes" and "Why
Choose JTH 3.5t Horseboxes" blocks from `app/(site)/page.tsx`. Fold the genuine

flagged by `UK_ENGLISH_COMPLIANCE.md` in passing.

**2.4 Quantify the facts.** `components/TrustIndicators.tsx` is already the right
pattern. Once figures are approved, swap in countable ones — number of boxes
built, years, the quality-check count if one exists.

### Phase 3 — Proof and provenance

**3.1 Build record.** Design a one-page build document issued with every box:
build number, chassis, dates, specification as delivered, the names of the team,
signed. Needs a template and a workshop process, not code. Then a short section
on `/about` and each model page explaining that every JTH comes with one.

**3.2 Owner photography.** Replace stock-feeling gallery captions in
`app/(site)/page.tsx` — the current ones ("Perfect balance of quality and
value", "Versatile and practical horsebox") describe the product rather than the
moment. Credit owners and yards where permission allows.

**3.3 Model page detail parity.** deVOL justify their flagship by naming
specific details — a Georgian bead, tongue-and-groove backboards, colour
matching. Aeos pages should do the same: name three or four things an Aeos has
that an Essential does not, concretely. That is what makes the Essential range
feel like a *choice* rather than a *compromise*, and it is what makes the Aeos
premium legible rather than asserted.

### Phase 4 — Longer term

Collaboration or limited edition, on the Holland Cooper × Bloomfields model.
Owners' register. A proper photographic commission — the single highest-leverage
spend available, since eight of the seventeen models in `pricing.json` currently
have no photography or a hero shot only, and no amount of copy survives a missing
or broken image.

## Part 5 — Two cautionary tales

**British Standard, by Plain English.** Launched 2012 as sensible cupboards at
sensible prices — same quality, fewer options, bought online. It worked
commercially and was well reviewed. It has since been discontinued, with the
business choosing to focus on growing Plain English instead.

The lesson is not "don't build a second range". It is that a second range run as
a *separate brand*, with its own name, its own site and its own buying model,
builds equity that never returns to the parent — and eventually the parent has to
choose. deVOL's structure avoids this: one maker's name over both ranges, so
every sale at either price point feeds the same name. **JTH should follow deVOL,
not British Standard** — which means resisting any temptation to give the second
range its own site or its own visual identity.

**The adjacent-claim trap.** Bloomfields lead with innovation over imitation.
JTH's homepage currently says it does not follow other manufacturers but leads
through innovation. These are the same claim, and Bloomfields got there first and
backs it with testable numbers. Claiming a rival's territory in weaker terms is
worse than saying nothing. JTH's defensible ground is different and better:
*family, workshop-visible, Norfolk-built, two ranges from one bench*. Own that.
